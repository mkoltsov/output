Campaign & armory

LAST SIGNAL · Campaign, progression and the armory

Product direction

A match should be a chapter in humanity's resistance. The player chooses an operation, studies the machine commander, equips a weapon and a starting kit, fights for an explicit objective, and returns to the campaign with a permanent record and new equipment. The six-operation sequence builds on the playable v0.2 tactical core, retro art and original score.

The complete intended path is campaign → loadout → mission → result → reward → next operation. Skirmish remains available for an independent match. Progress is local to this installation/browser origin; no account, online inventory or storefront purchase is required.

This chapter documents the v0.3 design and implementation contract. The verification chapter records actual integrated execution, so module presence or an engine simulation is not presented as campaign UI acceptance.

Research behind the progression choices

The publisher-authored description of Tactical Breach Wizards describes a campaign, distinct opponents, ability perks, freely reversible tactical experiments, and cosmetic outfits unlocked through optional objectives. Its account of outfit rewards explicitly separates appearance from combat power.

Our interpretation: retain undo, expose optional mastery challenges, show exactly what a clear unlocks, and keep armour skins cosmetic. LAST SIGNAL uses a different rules system and a much smaller six-operation structure. It does not reproduce that game's confidence currency, characters or spell system.

The publisher description of SteamWorld Heist II connects weapon and equipment choices to tactical roles and mission preparation. Its customization includes weapons, abilities and jobs.

Our interpretation: the carbine, railgun and arc projector need different legal targets, costs and effects. A gun cannot merely be a different card image with identical behaviour. LAST SIGNAL does not implement ricochet aiming, individual crew levels or that game's large equipment catalogue.

The earlier Into the Breach research also supports the connection between compact battles, equipment and understandable consequences. LAST SIGNAL applies that lesson through exact affected-sector previews. Enemy intent is not telegraphed in advance.

These publisher sources were read directly through Steam's public application-details endpoint on 31 October 2026. They inform design judgments. They do not establish that this implementation has equivalent balance, campaign length, commercial scope or player retention.

Six operations with real differences

All operations use the same 58-sector spatial language. Difficulty comes from the adversary's behaviour, forward deployment, reserves, weapon and objective. Later adversaries have declared advantages; campaign asymmetry is intentional and must be described before deployment.

02 · Supply Line SWARM Win and hold the central relay at (0,0) at settlement Convert 3 enemies in one ordinary move Within 100 turns 150
03 · Iron Choir BULWARK Win the operation Capture resource sites at least 10 times Within 95 turns 175
04 · Night Circuit HUNTER Win the operation Convert 4 enemies in one ordinary move Within 90 turns 225
05 · Relay Siege ARCHON Win and hold relays at (0,0) and (2,0) at settlement Capture resource sites at least 14 times Within 90 turns 275
06 · The Last Signal CORE Win the final operation Finish with at least 36 controlled sectors Within 85 turns 350

A completed turn counts either faction's ordinary turn and a forced pass. It is not a full human-plus-machine round. The speed target is a bonus criterion, not an invisible defeat timer.

A clear earns one star. Meeting the speed budget adds one, and the mastery challenge adds one, to a maximum of three. Failure or a draw earns no clear. Winning the territorial contest while missing a required relay also fails that mission. The result must name the missing objective rather than award campaign progress for the board winner alone.

Resource-capture challenges count actual acquisition rewards from deposits, including reclaiming a previously held deposit. They do not mean unique sites and do not count the +3 salvage-cache reward as a resource-site capture. Conversion challenges count one ordinary move, excluding EMP and gun actions. The HUD should distinguish the primary relay requirement, the optional challenge and the speed star.

Learning curve in v0.3.1

The first two operations use Recon. Recon prefers quiet expansion, considers a simple single-sector attack only periodically, and has no support for its first four turns. It can then use support at most every third turn, favouring shields and safe Drop Pods. It does not combine an EMP with a move; occasional single-target fire is introduced later. Warfront grants two quiet opening turns and uses support at most every other turn. Extinction retains full tactical support frequency and reply search.

The AI follows the same legal actions and costs. These are explicit differences in decision quality and cadence, with no secret resource gifts to the player. Later operations preserve their stronger adversaries and objectives. Existing saves keep their battlefield and equipment; the new decision policy applies on subsequent AI turns. New operation setup uses the revised starting reserves.

Adversary escalation

  • SENTRY introduces the city and economy with a conservative opponent and no extra forward units.
  • SWARM introduces the central relay on Recon. It starts with the same three sectors and no extra mission reserves; its Quartermaster kit and safe-deployment preference distinguish it from SENTRY.
  • BULWARK introduces Warfront with a forward alloy position and a reduced +2 mission alloy reserve, supporting a fortified network.
  • HUNTER carries a railgun and extra power, challenging a player who relies only on shields and clustered positions.
  • ARCHON carries an arc projector and a forward position. Its relay objective forces the player to care about where the battle ends.
  • CORE combines an arc projector, a forward unit and reinforced reserves. The final challenge rewards a decisive territorial win.

The AI uses persona-specific choices as well as those starting differences. All actions still pass through the same immutable rules, targeting, support cap and victory settlement. The enemy does not receive an unannounced extra turn or ignore its resource costs.

Loadout: one weapon, one starting kit, one skin

Equipment is chosen before deployment. The armory shows the selected item's effect, availability and unlock condition. The chosen loadout is validated against the profile at launch. Switching a profile loadout affects a new operation; it must not silently rewrite an active saved battlefield.

Three guns, three decisions

The arc's second target is selected deterministically by the engine; the preview shows the exact affected sectors. It is not a random chain. A railgun's shield penetration is deliberate and visible. The carbine is cheaper and directly useful; unlocking a larger gun introduces another tactical option rather than an automatic damage multiplier.

Gunfire is the equipped weapon's support action. It shares the same one support action per turn allowance as Aegis, EMP and Drop Pod, then normally leaves the ordinary deployment/relocation available. A shot can end the battle immediately, so the controller must check settlement before offering another move. Shots represent suppressing the machine presence and reclaiming the sector, consistent with the territorial fiction. There is no hidden hit chance, health bar, ammunition store or first-person aiming mode.

The board uses distinct carbine bursts, a focused rail streak and arc electricity. The armory uses original weapon illustrations. A selected rifle is not advertised as a new playable unit class or a full 3D equipment model.

Starting abilities

All three kits are available from the beginning. This makes the first loadout a real choice rather than presenting a screen full of locks.

Kit Starting resources One-time ability
Engineer +3 ALLOY A free Aegis on one starting corner, using normal expiry rules and no purchased support action
Saboteur +4 POWER First EMP costs 4 POWER instead of 8
Quartermaster +4 SUPPLIES First Drop Pod costs 3 SUPPLIES instead of 6

The base Survival bank is 2 of each resource. Kit resources are credited once when a new equipped battle is created. A used discount stays used in the saved battle. Reloading, reopening the armory or normalizing a profile must never issue the resource gift or charge again. Tactical/Classic mode has no economy or kit effects.

Engineer encourages a defensive anchor; Saboteur prepares a concentrated breakthrough; Quartermaster accelerates early territory and salvage access. Choosing a starting kit changes the opening plan while preserving free ordinary movement.

Four armour skins

Vanguard is the original Resistance look. Outlander unlocks after Dead Air, Ghost after Iron Choir, and Ember after the final operation. The variants change the displayed human armour palette in both preview and battlefield. Cyan ownership rings remain consistent, and NEXUS stays identifiable as the enemy.

These are runtime palette variants of the original generated human unit, not four separately modelled characters. Their rules, targeting, costs and starting strength are identical. The UI must say cosmetic only.

Progress that persists without farming

The profile records first-clear XP, best stars, best completed-turn count, and the chosen loadout. Operations unlock in order, with previously cleared operations available for replay. Weapons and skins unlock at named clear milestones. XP is a record of campaign progress; there is no shop, purchasable currency or invented spending function.

A first successful clear awards its XP once. Replaying or reopening results cannot issue it again. A replay may improve best stars or best turn count. A failed operation does not unlock the next one. The campaign and active battle have separate versioned storage records, and the v0.1/v0.2 save records remain separate.

A profile is local game data, not an anti-cheat service. No claim is made that a player cannot edit their own browser storage. The normal interface validates unlocks and avoids duplicate rewards.

Presentation and feedback

The title retains the painted retro cover. Campaign opens a complete operation hub with a six-step route, adversary identity, objective, rewards, earned stars, and a concrete reason for any lock. The armory presents large weapon art, kit descriptions, the human skin preview and the actual equipped state. Deploy launches the chosen operation rather than a generic match with a changed heading.

The live HUD identifies the mission and adversary, marks required relays, shows objective progress and displays the equipped weapon's cost and targeting state. Actual accepted gun actions trigger the matching new audio cue. A real unlock triggers a short original arcade fanfare. The result names the operation, pass/fail condition, stars, XP, unlocked equipment and the next available operation.

Repeated failure should leave a clear action: retry with another kit, replay an earlier operation, change available weapon, or return to skirmish. There is no permanent loss, paid continue, random equipment drop or mandatory grinding gate.

Balance and verification plan

The engine checks cover legal gun targets, shield penetration, deterministic arc selection, shared support limits, terminal shots, one-time kit effects, profile normalization, locked loadout validation and idempotent rewards. Legal winning routes should use equipment available at that mission's point in the sequence.

The application check follows the entire visible route from a fresh profile: choose a kit and skin, launch the first mission, use the equipped weapon, finish the operation, observe the actual result/reward, return to the hub, and confirm progression survives reload. Later missions require explicit relay outcomes. The exact missions/loadouts completed through UI and through direct engine execution are listed separately in verification.

These checks establish a functioning progression loop and playable routes. They do not establish that every kit has equal win rate, that all three-star targets are tuned for new players, or that six missions provide a particular number of hours. Human playtesting remains necessary for those conclusions.

Design & research

HEXXAGO: LAST SIGNAL

The playable promise

The last human city is a 58-sector battlefield. Deploy Resistance teams, reclaim districts from NEXUS, salvage power and supplies, and create one decisive breach before the machines surround you. The strategy remains easy to read: deploy one hex, relocate two hexes, take adjacent enemy sectors. The presentation combines painted 1980s science-fiction cover art, an arcade command terminal, physical miniature units, blue electrical effects, red machine optics, and an original synth/chiptune score.

The v0.2 presentation replaced the earlier country-versus-country archipelago direction. The current v0.3 adds a six-operation campaign, an armory, equipped weapons, starting kit abilities and persistent local progression; the campaign chapter specifies that layer. Humanity and NEXUS share the spatial rules and support-action rules. The base/skirmish board, deposits and caches are symmetric, with equal unmodified starting counts and banks; equipped kits apply their stated bonuses. Campaign missions deliberately vary enemy forward units, reserves, weapons and AI personas. Their silhouettes, voices in the interface, colours and sound treatment establish the fiction. Territory changing colour means a district has been reclaimed or occupied; humans do not physically turn into robots.

Research and interpretation

Research was conducted on 30–31 October 2026 through the existing managed browser. The links below distinguish facts established by a source from this project's design interpretation. No film footage, soundtrack recording, commercial game asset or DOS binary is included.

What to preserve from Hexxagon

The Internet Archive catalogue describes the 1993 game, its 58 spaces, three starting pieces per side, adjacent cloning, two-space jumps and conversion around the destination. Old-Games also describes the core rules, local two-player mode and difficulty choices.

Design conclusion: preserve that compact spatial language. Basic movement is free. Powerups are optional preparation before movement, so a player who spends their bank can still play a useful turn. A move can convert several neighbours at once, supplying the natural spectacle and comeback potential that the new presentation amplifies.

What the engine and distribution reference establishes

Phaser's HEXSTORM feature, the developer's browser offering, and its Steam listing establish a Phaser game with browser discovery and desktop distribution. They do not establish its exact engine version or desktop wrapper.

Our implementation choice: Phaser 3.90.0, TypeScript and Vite; the same built game in a local Electron shell. The browser ZIP follows itch.io's HTML5 packaging requirements. Steam publication still requires the developer account, store preparation, build review and other steps in Valve's onboarding documentation. A GitHub Pages release is a playable demonstration of this route; it is not an itch or Steam listing.

Human survival needs visible stakes

The publisher description of Terminator: Dark Fate – Defiance describes humanity fighting a synthetic machine network, limited resources, the importance of preserving units, and consequential urban tactics.

Design interpretation: use a ruined inhabited city, a human face in the menu, a massive red machine optic, scarce visible resources, and a clear survival directive. The threat should be recognizable before reading the rules. LAST SIGNAL remains a compact turn-based board game. Campaign persistence was outside the original v0.2 scope and is now implemented locally in v0.3; destructible buildings and real-time combined arms remain outside the scope.

Spectacle must preserve tactical clarity

Into the Breach's publisher-authored Steam description connects humanity's survival to small turn-based encounters, defending cities, and clearly telegraphed enemy attacks.

Design interpretation: highlight valid destinations, show the exact adjacent conversion area, distinguish protected enemies, and expose a power's cost and legal targets before purchase. The player should understand a dramatic event as a consequence of their decision. Our opponent chooses after the player moves; LAST SIGNAL does not implement Into the Breach's enemy-intent system. Effects communicate real state changes and should never imply damage or a hidden attack roll.

Combine dread with the possibility of rescue

The official Back to the Future page identifies the 1985 film, Alan Silvestri's composition credit, and a time-travel story built around a dangerous chain reaction. Its publicly displayed promotional art and imagery provide the visual reference for this brief.

Art interpretation: electric blue arcs, amber firelight, chrome-style title lettering, painted cover composition and a courageous central human give the city more emotional range than unbroken horror. The victory state turns toward warm light and the phrase HUMANITY ENDURES. Blue electrical trails are the game's relocation/EMP visual language; a time-travel campaign mechanic is not advertised. The game uses original generic people and machines, with no recognizable vehicle, film logo, actor likeness or copied melody.

Why the score uses machines as instruments

In his primary interview with Brian Reitzell, Brad Fiedel discusses hand-synchronized early sequencers, recording one part at a time, separating percussion and sustained parts between Fairlight systems, and the close relationship between music and sound effects.

Audio interpretation: layer an insistent low synth pulse, metallic percussion, gated snare, moving arpeggios and sustained harmonic tension. Add pulse-wave leads and noise percussion for arcade memory, then release tension with an original hopeful victory phrase. The result is a modern stereo hybrid, not a claim of exact vintage hardware emulation. All notes and sound sources are generated for this project.

Research limitations

The Subset Games site and NESdev APU page returned browser security checks. Several guessed documentation links returned 404 pages. Those pages were not used as technical evidence. Phaser API choices are checked against the installed 3.90.0 source and types, with the versioned source as the reproducible reference. The nostalgic art and engagement conclusions above are design judgments, not proof from a player study.

The first minute

  1. Arrival: full-screen cover art, LAST SIGNAL title, a short human-survival premise, and one dominant CAMPAIGN button. The six-operation hub leads to loadout and deployment; Skirmish remains an independent match. Music awaits interaction.
  2. Deployment: the city board appears with three cyan human teams. The base/skirmish starts with three red machine units; campaign missions can add declared forward units and reserves. Territory and the three resource banks remain visible.
  3. First decision: select a unit. Adjacent deployment and two-hex relocation use distinct highlights. Hover or focus reveals the destination and conversion count.
  4. First reward: a captured deposit pays immediately. A salvage crate gives a larger one-time payout. The HUD shows the actual resource change.
  5. First reversal: NEXUS moves under the same rules. The player sees sector conversion and can plan a shield, an EMP, a Drop Pod or equipped weapon fire.

The menu contains working Campaign, Armory, Continue, Skirmish, instructions and settings choices. There are no inactive multiplayer lobby, shop or online-account buttons.

Core rules

  • 58 playable sectors on a radius-four axial hex board, with three fixed holes.
  • The base/skirmish starts with three units per side in alternating corners. Campaign missions can add declared enemy forward units. Resistance moves first.
  • DEPLOY / 1 HEX: select an owned sector and an empty adjacent sector. The original remains occupied, creating one additional controlled sector.
  • RELOCATE / 2 HEXES: move to an empty sector at hex distance two. The original becomes empty. Intervening sectors do not block the move.
  • After either ordinary action, all unshielded enemy sectors adjacent to the destination change ownership. No chain propagation beyond those neighbours occurs.
  • A shield does not block movement to another empty sector. It prevents conversion of its own occupied sector.
  • A player with no legal ordinary move passes automatically. If both sides cannot move, the match settles.

The ending conditions are elimination, full occupation, neither side able to move, three repetitions of the same ownership/active-player/shield pattern (excluding banks and remaining caches), or 180 completed turns. Territory is the primary score. In Survival, total banked resources break a territory tie. A further tie is a draw. A terminal action settles before recurring round income.

Survival economy

The HUD names resources POWER, ALLOY and SUPPLIES. Their internal serialized keys remain energy, ore and food so the proven rules interface can be extended without a wholesale rewrite.

The unmodified Survival bank is 2 of every resource per faction. Equipped starting kits add their stated resources and one-time abilities; campaign setup can add declared enemy reserves. Claiming a resource sector pays 1 matching resource immediately. Each completed nonterminal round, after NEXUS's turn, every owned resource sector pays 1 more. Capturing an opponent's deposit transfers its future income and pays the new owner's acquisition reward.

Ordinary deployment and relocation cost nothing. Powers spend only earned resources. There is no real-money currency or random reward roll.

Core support powers

The three original v0.2 powers remain available. In v0.3, the equipped gun adds weapon fire to the same support allowance; weapon effects and starting-kit discounts are specified in the campaign chapter. The table below gives standard costs before those discounts.

One optional support action per turn, before the ordinary move. Aegis, EMP, Drop Pod and equipped weapon fire share that allowance. A purchase normally preserves the ordinary move; if it ends the match or leaves no legal move, the engine settles or passes immediately.

Power Cost Legal target Exact effect Tactical role and counterplay
Aegis Shield 3 ALLOY Any owned, unshielded sector Protect that sector through the next opposing turn; expires when that turn finishes Preserve a central anchor or resource district. Opponent can attack elsewhere or relocate around it. Relocating the protected unit vacates its protection.
EMP Pulse 8 POWER Owned sector beside at least one unshielded enemy Reclaim all unshielded enemy sectors immediately adjacent to that owned sector Break an encirclement, then use the normal move to exploit it. Aegis-protected sectors resist the pulse. No extra combo reward is granted for EMP conversion.
Drop Pod 6 SUPPLIES Empty sector adjacent to any owned sector Add one controlled sector without converting its neighbours Establish another launch point, claim salvage, or occupy a critical empty sector before the normal move. Enemy adjacency alone does not create a free conversion.

Costs are deliberately different. Alloy buys a short defensive commitment. Power buys a high-impact conversion or weapon fire. Supplies buy position and one extra body. They compete for the same support allowance, preventing a single turn from stacking purchased powers into an unreadable cascade.

An EMP reclaiming two enemy sectors changes the territory difference by four: two leave the enemy and join the player. Spending eight power for that swing can be useful, but a shielded anchor can stop the best target. A drop pod adds one territory before the ordinary move and can open another destination. A shield has no immediate territory reward; its value depends on what the enemy can reclaim. These tradeoffs create choices that a simple banked-score economy lacked.

Salvage and arcade bonuses

Six visible single-use caches are placed symmetrically: two power, two alloy and two supply caches. Each grants +3 of its resource to the first faction claiming the sector. The cache disappears permanently. Reclaiming the sector later does not recreate it. These are placed objectives, not random drops.

Ordinary movement grants a conversion bonus in addition to acquisition rewards:

Converted neighbours Bonus Feedback
0–2 No combo bonus Ordinary sector/capture response
3–4 +2 POWER OVERRUN! with a short arcade cue
5–6 +4 POWER and +2 SUPPLIES CHAIN REACTION! with a stronger burst

The upper bonus replaces the lower one. Shields reduce the real conversion count. EMPs and gunfire do not generate combo rewards. The source records earned rewards, and the interface renders those records; visual celebration is never random. Symmetry describes the base board and resource/cache placement, not the deliberately asymmetric campaign deployments or proof of first-player balance.

Engagement rhythm

The intended rhythm is spot a resource → choose a route → earn salvage → choose one power → create a multi-sector swing → see the enemy answer. Caches produce early objectives, recurring deposits produce a middle-game contest, and finite space creates an endgame. Large captures combine visible tactical success, sound and useful resources.

Avoid giving the losing faction an unexplained hidden advantage. Both sides use the same tools and cost rules; equipped weapons and kit abilities determine their available actions, and campaign advantages come from the declared mission setup. The current build has no daily streak, loot box or mandatory grind. Campaign clears unlock weapons and cosmetic skins and preserve best stars and turn counts; they do not increase a permanent damage statistic. Replay can pursue a different loadout, a missed star or another tactical approach. Balance and long-term replay value need human play sessions; complete AI matches demonstrate rule progress, not player enjoyment.

Modes and opponent

Survival uses deposits, caches, powers and combo rewards. Tactical preserves the plain spatial contest without the economy. Dead City spreads income across the perimeter and approach routes. Ground Zero adds central resource pressure using the same board topology. Local duel lets two people control the factions on one device; no network connection is implied.

The v0.3 Campaign links six operations over these battlefields, with distinct adversaries, primary relay objectives in two missions, optional star challenges, first-clear XP and equipment unlocks. Its declared enemy setups are asymmetric. Skirmish retains the shared base setup and selectable difficulty.

Three AI choices are presented as Recon, Warfront and Extinction. v0.3.1 makes Recon the default for new skirmishes and the first two campaign operations. Recon uses forgiving expansion choices and delayed, infrequent support; Warfront introduces more frequent support after its opening grace period; Extinction retains its full tactical search. The AI evaluates affordable support actions through the same engine. After an EMP, Drop Pod or shot it replans movement; after a shield it can retain a still-valid plan. Difficulty alone does not add money or hidden rules; campaign metadata separately supplies the declared enemy resources, deployments and personas.

Visual and menu specification

Main menu

Use the painted key art full bleed. The left portion stays dark enough for a large chrome LAST and fiery SIGNAL title. One amber CAMPAIGN control anchors the composition, with Armory, Continue and Skirmish available. A compact top bar holds sound and settings; the footer signals a functioning human transmission. CRT scanlines, restrained grain, embers and terminal labels supply texture. They must not obscure the title, action or focused control.

Active battlefield

The city backdrop supports a real Phaser board of raised dark sector platforms. Human and machine cutouts have distinct physical silhouettes, supported by cyan/red ownership rings and text. Resource and cache marks stay legible beside the units. The selected unit, ordinary destinations, protected sectors and power targets require separate shapes or marks, so colour is not the sole cue.

Real movement produces landing feedback. Sector reclamation produces directional energy, sparks and an ownership transition. EMP uses a blue electrical shock ring; Aegis uses a contained protective outline; Drop Pod uses a vertical arrival streak. Routine resource, cache and combo rewards update a small HUD line outside the board. They do not produce floating text or screen-covering cards. Camera shake is disabled. Reduced motion also removes large travelling effects while retaining state changes and labels.

Command HUD and results

The board occupies the centre. Faction panels provide a portrait, territory, resource banks and the active side. The action tray exposes power costs and availability. Field intelligence explains the selected action and real recent events. A compact command menu offers setup, instructions, audio, undo, surrender and return to the title.

Skirmish victory says HUMANITY ENDURES; human defeat says SIGNAL LOST. Campaign debriefs use SIGNAL RESTORED or MISSION FAILED, with the primary objective, stars, XP and new unlocks. Local-duel results identify the actual winning faction. Operation completion updates a compact inline result strip and leaves the battlefield visible. The full debrief opens only through an explicit Review result control; rewards are still settled immediately and once. Enemy support is rendered before its ordinary move, with a short separation between the two actions. Settings expose mute, music, volume and reduced motion. Keyboard access covers ordinary moves and support targets.

Audio specification

Two original loop states separate title tension from active combat. The menu is slower and spacious, with threatening bass and a hopeful upper motif. Battle adds a faster pulse, denser percussion, sharper FM/pulse lead and arpeggio movement. Menu-to-battle transitions crossfade after audio unlock. The thirteen v0.2 cues cover selection, deployment, relocation, capture, resource, victory, defeat, invalid action, start, shield, EMP, reinforcement and combo. The v0.3 campaign adds carbine, railgun, arc, unlock and mission cues, for eighteen discrete effects.

This is a modern analog/chiptune hybrid. The brief evokes the technological optimism and menace of 1980s–1990s science fiction, without pretending to meet a specific console's channel or sample limits. See the audio manifest for actual tempo, durations, generation method, levels and file hashes.

  4. Generate original music and cues; fetch and decode the emitted files in the running browser and desktop shell.
  5. Exercise whole matches, powers, AI turn transitions, undo and save/continue through the integrated application.
  6. Package the browser build and separate native application; publish reviewed non-company artifacts and read back the live release.

Weapon and mission rendering implementation

The actual scene reads equipped weapon/skin from GameState.loadouts. Shot target previews call the engine's getPowerupEffects to show the exact affected sectors, including the one valid chained ARC sector and railgun penetration of protected targets. Actual lastAction shot records trigger firing from the nearest friendly unit: amber carbine muzzle/streaks, a cyan penetrating rail, or purple/cyan electrical links. Converted sectors come from the actual action record. No damage number, extra converted target or unearned reward is invented by rendering. Reduced-motion mode shows the resolved board and a brief static event label.

Mission objective sectors from missionTargets receive a star, vertical beacon and RELAY label separate from resource/cache symbols. The scene does not decide whether a mission is cleared. Mission settlement remains an engine/campaign decision.

3. Synthesis and reuse

The generator is:

scripts/generate-last-signal-audio.py

The earlier generator was inspected. Its useful principles were retained: MIDI-to-frequency calculation, equal-power stereo placement, seeded procedural generation and circularly wrapped event/delay tails. Its soft wood/glass/sine voices did not provide the requested pulse bass, industrial drums, FM effects or dense score. The new implementation therefore extends that approach with vector synthesis and filtering.

The generator uses:

  • PolyBLEP-corrected saw and pulse edges rather than naïve hard-edged oscillators.
  • Several duty cycles, including a narrow chip pulse.
  • Harmonic-limited triangle and sub-sine layers.
  • Detuned saw ensembles for brass.
  • Frequency/phase modulation for metallic notes, hacks and sweeps.
  • Seeded noise, filters and enveloped oscillators for every drum and impact.

This is offline asset generation. The application does not synthesize a new soundtrack during play, contact a music service or require a model/API key.

Gesture and pending-load behavior

  • Only unlock creates or resumes an AudioContext.
  • Call unlock directly from a real click/key gesture; it starts resume before any await.
  • startMusic records music intent without creating a context.
  • setScene records the desired scene even before tracks decode.
  • Each individually decoded file reconciles pending work; the first usable effect/music does not wait for every unrelated sample.
  • One most recent effect may wait for its buffer. It is consumed once, discarded when muted, and expires after 1.5 seconds to avoid replaying stale actions after a long denied/suspended interval.
  • play, unlock, stop and destroy do not throw an audio failure through a game action.

The UI may default its music preference to enabled. That does not promise autoplay before browser activation. Calling startMusic before unlock is supported and silent until a usable context exists.

Music scenes and levels

Use menu for home/setup and battle for an active game. Returning to menu or results can select menu again.

New tracks fade in and prior tracks fade out over 0.7 seconds. The old source is explicitly retired/disconnected; repeated requests for the active scene do not add another loop. A fallback is provided for contexts without cancelAndHoldAtTime.

The manager's default master value is 0.55. The UI can set its preference, with 0.45–0.60 a sensible initial range for this bank. Music uses a separate 0.58 voice gain; effects use 0.82, with victory/defeat at 0.72. A high-threshold compressor catches rare overlapping action peaks without being the mechanism that normalizes the files.

Muting affects the shared master. Music can continue silently in phase; unmuting does not need to start a duplicate loop.

Lifecycle and cleanup

stopMusic clears music intent and stops every current/fading music voice. A later fetch/decode completion cannot restart it.

destroy aborts fetches, clears the queued event, stops/disconnects music and effects, clears fade-cleanup timers and buffers, detaches context callbacks and catches the close promise. Late asynchronous completions check both destruction and context identity.

SFX are not allowed to accumulate while output is suspended. Scene changes or missing optional files never mutate the game state.
